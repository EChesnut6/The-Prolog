Visual References in The Lighthouse

By Eli Chesnut • 2026-06-28

Mythological Foundations

Robert Eggers' The Lighthouse is a visual marvel, filmed in a boxy 1.19:1 aspect ratio with high-contrast black-and-white orthochromatic-like imagery. While the film presents a gritty tale of two lighthouse keepers descending into madness, it is visually and narratively anchored in classical mythology and fine art.

The most prominent mythological reference is the tale of Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire from the gods and was sentenced to eternal torment. Robert Pattinson's character, Winslow, is driven by an obsession with the lighthouse's light—the divine fire. The final, horrifying image of Winslow lying on the rocks while seagulls peck at his organs is a direct, visceral recreation of Prometheus' punishment.

Painting Inspirations

Eggers and cinematographer Jarin Blaschke drew heavy inspiration from Symbolist and Romantic painters to capture the movie's haunting, dreamlike atmosphere:

1. Sascha Schneider's Hypnosis (1904)

One of the most striking visual matches in the film is Sascha Schneider’s painting Hypnosis. The painting depicts a nude man standing in a state of trance or submission before a giant, stylized figure whose shadow towers over him. This stark, vertical alignment and hypnotic dominance is mirrored exactly in the staging of Willem Dafoe's character, Thomas Wake, standing over Winslow.

2. Jean Delville's The Treasures of Satan (1895)

The depiction of the sea monster—complete with tentacles, tentacles wrapped around Winslow, and the eroticized, terrifying nature of the sea's mysteries—draws inspiration from Jean Delville and other fin-de-siècle Symbolists. The sea is not just water; it is a living, breathing, mythological entity.

3. Gustave Doré's Engravings

The dense, heavy blacks and atmospheric shadows in the indoor scenes of the lighthouse tower are directly reminiscent of Gustave Doré’s classical illustrations for Dante's Inferno and Coleridge's The Rime of the Ancient Mariner. The light does not just illuminate; it casts shadows that feel physically heavy, trapping the characters in their own psychological prison.
