The Pre-View

What to know before watching

The Premise

David Aames, a young wealthy publishing tycoon in NYC, appears to have it all-except the right girl. His carefree life is incomplete without someone truly special to share it with, but as he attempts to navigates his lovelife, the rest of reality starts to cave in.

Essentials

Vanilla Sky is hard to pigeonhole into one genre, because it just isn't one genre, or even two. The movie has a somewhat drastic tone and genre change, moving from a classic romance to a psychological thriller with a splash of sci-fi mixed in. As a result, the film demands the viewer qusetion what is real while watching. I do suggest watching the trailer and stopping at 1:22. From which you'll have gathered the premise and just a touch more without spoiling anything important.

Remix

Vanilla Sky is actually a remake of the Spanish movie Abre los ojos (Open Your Eyes), which came out in 1997. Cameron Crowe, the director of the former, considers his version more of a remix rather than a true remake. Alejandro Amenábar has also praised the work of Crowe, comparing them as,

"the same song sung by very different voices, one more operatic while the other is rock and roll." — Alejandro Amenábar

A fun fact about Abre los ojos is that Penelope Cruz actually plays the same character, Sofia, in both. In the case that you've already seen Open Your Eyes, then you will perhaps have a deeper appreciation and notice a dialog between the two films which Crowe has mentioned in multiple interviews as being purposeful.

Keep in Mind

Pay special attention to the use of commercial music. Crowe has noted in interviews that the soundtrack was chosen specifically to comment directly on the characters' state as well as the film itself. Some songs are also used in an ironic sense, being turned on their head to juxtapose the current situation. Many of these songs were popular at the time or had been in the past, especially in commercials. Crowe loved the idea of using commercial and popular music in a nonconventional way.

From the Future

For viewers like myself who weren't around for the 2001 release, the real-life tabloid drama that was unfolding adds a strange and extra layer of tension and context regarding the romantic scenes. During filming in the year 2000, Tom Cruise was married to Nicole Kidman. It was during this filming that Tom Cruise met Penelope Cruz. In August of 2001, 4 months before Vanilla Sky was set to release, Tom Cruise finalized his divorce with Nicole Kidman. It was that same month that his relationhsip with Penelope Cruz went public. Of course the tabloids went wild. "Was their acting out romance what led to Cruise and Kidman's divorce???" While this doesn't make a significant difference watching the film, it does add an extra layer of context which I think is fun if not important. For the record, Cruise and Cruz's highly publicized and scrutinized real-life relationship lasted about 3 years ending due to conflicting and demanding schedules.

As you can tell by my ratings of this, I generally liked this movie. I found the hard switch between the moods to be interesting. I liked the character of David as a complex protagonist.

The ending, and really the last 3rd, of the movie is where ideas thrive. That's not to say that the first 2/3rds don't have pleanty to analyze and go over, but relative the the final act, they lack discussion.

There are two main ways to look at this, either what we see and are told are the absolute truth. David has now woken up 150 years later. He took the procedure and everything from the night of the club after is a dream that went wrong. Or, another popular theory is that everything after the crash is a dream in a coma state.

Personally, I opt to believe it is any explanation other than the first. As I watched this movie I believed that it could not be explained away, like how it was, and it be true. I felt that it would mean the movie didn't have the balls to follow through, which I believe is not what happened, but that the supposed explanation was only meant as an inventation to look deeper. Which subsequently is what is asked of David in this film. He is questioning himself. He is in a coma and has created this dream where he torments himself regarding truth. Who is he? What and who does he value? He recognizes his lack of love from a parent. He recognizes his extreme vanity. He recognizes his struggle to let go of his childish ways and grow up. It's as is quoted and as Jules Verne says, "It's a revolution of the mind." He reconciles his fear of heights as he reconciles his fear of growing up and changing.

I think I believe this version of the ending because it keeps the film from falling apart in the third act, at least in my opinion.
